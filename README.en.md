# edurisk

*[Русская версия](README.md)*

Early identification of at-risk students from weekly LMS data: time series, derived features, explanations for the instructor, and ethical safeguards. The project is a graduation thesis (diploma) in progress. **All stages of the plan are done**: data, features, simulator, storage and an OULAD adapter, risk models, grades 2–5 and exam admission, explainability, fairness and privacy, an instructor interface. What is untested on real data and what could not be installed in the development environment is listed under "Limitations". The plan and statuses are in [PLAN.md](PLAN.md); an outline for the thesis text is in [docs/VKR_OUTLINE.md](docs/VKR_OUTLINE.md). Code comments, the UI and result files are in Russian.

**Important: every number below comes from synthetic data.** It shows that the pipeline works, not how well it performs on real students.

## What is here

- **`edurisk/simulate.py`: a cohort simulator with a known causal model.** Hidden ability and motivation, "loss of interest" in a random week, dropping out, checkpoints, an outcome. It exists to test whether the models and explanations find what was built into the data, which cannot be checked on real data.
- **`edurisk/features.py`: 20 weekly features** (static, raw aggregates, derived: a streak of failed checkpoints, activity trend, EWMA, weeks since last login, change in score, activity relative to the cohort).
- **`edurisk/curriculum.py` and `edurisk/world.py` (stage 2): a multi-course simulator.** A curriculum of several semesters with Russian forms of assessment: *zachet* (pass/fail), differentiated *zachet*, exam and coursework (grade 2–5), credit units (ZE) and course difficulty, prerequisites. A student goes through semesters in turn: motivation drifts, engagement is shared across a semester's courses plus a per-course "interest", failed checkpoints lower later engagement (feedback), exam admission depends on the share of checkpoints passed, no admission or dropout means grade "2", and a retake is stored separately and does not enter the label. Scenarios: S0 smooth, S1 threshold feedback ("learned helplessness"), S2 cohort shift, S3 group-dependent measurement bias (for fairness), S4 data loss. `World.course_view(c)` returns a course in the stage-1 format, `World.history_before(s)` only the results of earlier semesters, `World.labels()` the targets T1 (fail), T2 (grade), T3 (admission).
- **`edurisk/store.py`, `db/schema_pg.sql`, `docker-compose.yml` (stage 3): storage.** A 3NF schema (students, courses, checkpoint schedule, enrolments with results, weekly activity, checkpoints, an action log), database-level constraints (forms of assessment, grades 2–5, foreign keys). `save_world` / `load_world` give an exact round trip with `World`; `activity_until(conn, course, week)` is a week slice at the SQL level; `log_action` keeps a "who viewed what" log. The simulator's hidden truth is not stored in the database. Tests run on SQLite (standard library); the PostgreSQL DDL is generated by the same code (`python scripts/make_schema.py pg`).
- **`edurisk/oulad.py`: an adapter for the open OULAD dataset** (`python scripts/load_oulad.py <folder> [DB]`). Written from the dataset description and tested on a synthetic dataset in its format with hand-computed weeks, active days, clicks, late submissions and carried-over scores; **it has not been run on the real files**.
- **`dataset.py`, `costs.py`, `metrics.py`, `models.py`, `experiment.py` (stage 4): failure-risk models (T1).** A "student × course × week" sample (stage-1 features + history from earlier semesters + credit units and form of assessment), split 60/20/20 strictly by student. Models: the rule "2 failed in a row or mean score < 3", elastic-net logistic regression, gradient boosting, boosting with ZE and class weights, isotonic calibration on validation, stacking (weights learned on validation only). Metrics: ROC-AUC, PR-AUC, Brier, ECE, expected cost with ZE weight, weighted recall in the attention list (top 10% by p·w·k). Paired bootstrap over students with Holm correction.
- **`ordinal.py`, `conformal.py`, `experiment5.py` (stage 5): grades 2–5, admission, conformal sets.** T2: a Frank–Hall cascade (three binary models P(≥3), P(≥4), P(=5) with isotonic calibration and enforced monotonicity), multiclass logit and boosting, and a class-frequency baseline. Decisions use a cost matrix (overestimating a grade costs `asym` times more than underestimating, multiplied by the course's ZE weight): the grade with minimum expected cost instead of argmax. T3: probability of non-admission. Conformal sets of grades with a threshold set on validation, overall and per true class.
- **`explain.py` (stage 6): explainability.** Exact interventional Shapley values over five meaningful feature groups (activity, checkpoints, history, background, course) for any model; exact feature contributions of the logistic model; "what if" (replacing a group with typical values); grouped permutation importance; neutral texts for the instructor ("attention zone"). The SHAP library could not be installed in the environment, so this is implemented without it.
- **`fairness.py`, `privacy.py`, `docs/MODEL_CARD.md`, `docs/DATA_SHEET.md` (stage 7): fairness and ethics.** Per-group metrics (flag rate, TPR, FPR, AUC, ECE), differences with bootstrap intervals, per-group thresholds that equalise false alarms, pseudonymisation (HMAC), removal of sensitive columns, k-anonymity, roles and permissions, a model card and a data sheet.
- **`service.py`, `app.py`, `scripts/run_demo.py` (stage 8): the instructor interface.** A risk list per course with a priority (probability × ZE weight × cost), a student card (a text explanation, charts of logins and checkpoints, group contributions, "what if"), a "disagree" button, a log of views and disagreements, roles teacher / methodist / admin, signed tokens, CSRF protection on forms, HTML escaping, charts as inline SVG with no JavaScript. The web layer is WSGI + jinja2; FastAPI would not install in the environment, so the logic is kept apart from the web layer (`service.py`) to make porting easy. Run it with `python scripts/run_demo.py`.
- **Leakage prevention by construction.** Features for week *t* are computed from `cohort.observed(t)`, in which future data physically does not exist.

```python
from edurisk.simulate import simulate
from edurisk.features import build_features

cohort = simulate(1000, seed=0)
X = build_features(cohort, week=6)      # features from the first 6 weeks of data
y = cohort.outcome                      # True = failed the course or dropped out
```

## Verification

`python -m unittest discover -s tests`: 126 tests (stages 1–8), about 1.5 minutes. Requires numpy, pandas, scikit-learn.

- Vectorised features are compared, for each of the 16 weeks, with an independent brute-force implementation (loops over students).
- **Leakage test:** all data after week *t* is replaced with random values and the outcome is inverted; the week-*t* features must stay bit-for-bit the same. A paired sensitivity test checks that changing the past does change the features (otherwise the leakage test would mean nothing).
- Failure streaks, a missed checkpoint (counted as a failure), EWMA and trend are checked on hand-computed examples.
- `python scripts/mutate.py` introduces 103 typical bugs into the code (`--targeted` runs only the marked ones, with the affected test set), for example shifting a window one week ahead, flipped signs, a streak that does not reset, the admission boundary, dropped-out students who keep studying, history that includes the current semester; all are caught. Several mutations survived at first and led to new tests: a strict conformal-set boundary, cascade calibration on the training sample, ECE at a forecast of 1.0, calibration on training data, training without class weights, an SQL slice that includes an extra week, swapped fields when writing to the database, late submissions counted, an exam turned into a checkpoint, and one survivor caused by a weak OULAD test set (the set was fixed). One mutation turned out to be equivalent to the original code (a redundant multiplier was removed), and another survived only because of a luckily chosen α = 0.5, so α was changed to 0.4 and a hand-computed example was added.

### What the stage-2 tests check

Admission recomputed by an independent brute-force implementation on random student–course pairs; consistency of grades, passes, non-admission, dropout and retakes; no activity after leaving; outcome shares within realistic bounds (about 19% failures, about 14% dropout over 4 semesters, about 87% admission); causal signs (ability, course difficulty) and **recovery of the built-in prerequisite coefficient** by regression on hidden variables; history cannot see the current or later semesters; stage-1 features on `course_view` do not depend on data after week *t*; each scenario's own effect is tested (the feedback jump in S1, a model trained on cohort 0 underestimating the risk of cohort 1 in S2, a 25% login shift without a performance shift in S3, record loss without outcome change in S4). The first versions of the S2 and prerequisite tests were wrong and had to be rewritten: the hypothesis "the link between activity and outcome weakens" was not confirmed (it even strengthened, as the failure share grew), so what is tested is what actually matters, the calibration mismatch.

## Stage 2 result

`python scripts/stage2_check.py` ([docs/stage2_results.txt](docs/stage2_results.txt)): boosting on stage-1 features, trained on one seed and tested on another. ROC-AUC by form of assessment at weeks 4 / 8 / 12: zachet 0.83 / 0.91 / 0.99; differentiated zachet 0.84 / 0.94 / 0.99; exam 0.79 / 0.90 / 0.98; coursework 0.84 / 0.94 / 0.99. Cohort shift (S2, exam): a model trained on cohort 0 gives almost the same AUC on the new cohort (0.77 / 0.90 / 0.98) but underestimates the share at risk (forecast 0.19–0.25 against an actual 0.28). So **ranking is preserved but calibration breaks**, and thresholds will need to be recomputed.

**Caveat:** the label "did not pass on the first attempt" includes non-admission, and admission is almost entirely determined by checkpoints already known by week 12. An AUC around 0.99 in late weeks is therefore trivial; weeks 4–8, with AUC 0.79–0.94, are the meaningful ones.

## Stage 4 result

`python scripts/stage4_experiment.py` (about 3 minutes; full output in [docs/stage4_results.txt](docs/stage4_results.txt)). Five worlds of 2000 students, k = 3 (missing a failure costs 3 times a false alarm), ZE weight γ = 2. Mean over the five worlds (the file also has other metrics and the spread):

| week | model | ROC-AUC | cost |
|---|---|---|---|
| 4 | rule | 0.660 | 0.481 |
| 4 | logistic regression | 0.851 | 0.348 |
| 4 | boosting + calibration | 0.848 | 0.347 |
| 4 | stacking | 0.852 | 0.348 |
| 8 | rule | 0.702 | 0.449 |
| 8 | logistic regression | 0.924 | 0.251 |
| 8 | boosting + calibration | 0.922 | 0.249 |
| 8 | stacking | 0.924 | 0.249 |

(Flagging nobody costs 0.647; flagging everybody costs 0.892.)

Findings, including the unflattering ones:

1. **Trained models are far better than the rule** (ROC-AUC difference +0.19 at week 4 and +0.22 at week 8, Holm p < 0.05; cost lower by 0.13 and 0.20).
2. **Boosting is not better than logistic regression.** Differences are around 0.002–0.003 AUC, and the cost difference is not statistically significant. The reason is in the simulator: the dependence on features is almost additive, so boosting has no nonlinearity to find. On real data the conclusion may differ.
3. **Training with ZE weights gave no cost gain** (difference +0.002, p = 1). For the binary formulation this is expected: the course weight multiplies both errors, and the decision threshold 1/(1+k) does not depend on credit units. The weight changes the final cost estimate and the order in a limited attention list; the model itself is barely affected. The ZE-weighting idea from the prototype keeps its meaning for the multiclass formulation (stage 5), but not here.
4. **Calibration is mandatory after weighted training:** without it, the ECE of boosting with weights is 0.12 (week 4) and 0.08 (week 8); after isotonic calibration it is 0.015 and 0.011. Without weights calibration changes little, and PR-AUC drops slightly because neighbouring values are merged.
5. **Stacking** gives a ROC-AUC 0.003 higher than boosting (significant), but the difference from logistic regression is not significant. It is not worth the trouble.
6. Sensitivity to k and γ: the order of models is preserved in all nine combinations.

**Comparison with the prototype.** The prototype's sources are gone, so its metrics (Macro-F1 0.525, recall of class "2" 0.62) cannot be recomputed on this data: different data, a different formulation. The rule baseline plays the prototype's role here; an honest comparison is possible only once real data is available or the prototype is reconstructed from the paper.

## Stage 5 result

`python scripts/stage5_experiment.py` (about 10 minutes; [docs/stage5_results.txt](docs/stage5_results.txt)). Five worlds of 2000 students; T2 covers only differentiated zachets, exams and coursework (zachets have no grade), with about 20% in class "2" (including non-admission and dropout). Mean over worlds; the prototype's metrics by meaning (Macro-F1, recall of class "2") are shown alongside, but **on different data, so this is not a comparison**:

| week | model (argmax) | QWK | MAE | Macro-F1 | recall "2" | recall "2" with cost-based decision |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 4 | class-frequency baseline | 0.00 | 0.83 | 0.14 | 0.00 | 0.00 |
| 4 | cascade + boosting | 0.63 | 0.54 | 0.54 | 0.51 | 0.66 |
| 8 | class-frequency baseline | 0.00 | 0.83 | 0.14 | 0.00 | 0.00 |
| 8 | cascade + boosting | 0.73 | 0.44 | 0.61 | 0.68 | 0.80 |
| 12 | cascade + boosting | 0.86 | 0.28 | 0.74 | 0.88 | 0.94 |

1. **Deciding by minimum expected cost sharply raises the recall of failing grades** (from 0.51 to 0.66 at week 4, from 0.68 to 0.80 at week 8) and lowers cost by 0.33–0.43 (intervals far from zero). Caveat: the cost is measured with the same matrix used for the decision, so its reduction is expected; the metric-independent effect is the rise in recall of failing grades, bought with more cautious grade estimates.
2. **The Frank–Hall cascade is NOT better than a plain multiclass model.** By log loss it is worse by 0.01–0.03 (significant); by cost the difference is not significant. The ordinal structure did not pay off on this simulator. This is unexpected, and the finding should be re-checked on real data.
3. **ZE-weighted training of the cascade does not reduce cost** (differences within noise), as in stage 4. The influence of credit units stays in the cost estimate and the ordering of the attention list, not in training.
4. **Conformal sets with a single shared threshold give the desired ~90% on average but under-cover rare and "middle" classes:** class "2" 0.81 and class "3" 0.79 at week 4. A per-class threshold fixes this (0.94 / 0.89 / 0.90 / 0.90 by class) at the price of larger sets (2.6 grades against 2.3 at week 4, 2.1 against 1.9 at week 8). A warning system needs the per-class variant.
5. **Cohort shift (S2) did not break coverage** (0.918 against 0.900 for the "own" cohort; 0.90 for class "2"). I expected a drop. The shift in the simulator is moderate and moves probabilities uniformly, so the guarantee held; with a different kind of shift on real data it must be re-checked.
6. **T3, non-admission:** ROC-AUC 0.87 (week 4), 0.94 (week 8), 0.99 (week 12), with good calibration (ECE about 0.01). At week 12 this is an almost deterministic consequence of the checkpoints, as in stage 4.

## Stage 6 result: feature ablation and explainability

`scripts/stage6_experiment.py` ([docs/stage6_results.txt](docs/stage6_results.txt)), `scripts/stage6_explain_refined.py` ([docs/stage6_explain_refined.txt](docs/stage6_explain_refined.txt)).

**Ablation (ROC-AUC; scenarios S0, S1 threshold feedback, S4 data loss; 4 worlds of 1200 students).** Feature sets: context (background, history, course), + raw aggregates, + derived, everything. Week 8, boosting, S0: context 0.776; + raw 0.921; + derived 0.919; all 0.922.

1. Behavioural features provide the main gain (+0.14 to +0.16 AUC at week 8).
2. **The hypothesis "derived features (failure streaks, trends) improve accuracy" was not confirmed in any scenario**, including the threshold S1 where it was expected: derived features without raw ones are even slightly worse than raw (−0.002 to −0.008), and adding them to raw ones gives +0.002–0.003 AUC (significant but practically negligible). This agrees with the stage-1 result: boosting itself reconstructs sums and streaks from aggregates. Derived features stay useful as formulations that are **understandable to the instructor** ("3 checkpoints in a row not passed"), but not as a source of accuracy.
3. Explanations. The first version of the simulator check **did not confirm the expectation**: for students with low ability, the share of behavioural groups in positive contributions was no lower than for students who lost interest. There are two reasons. First, low ability shows up through failed checkpoints (their scores depend on ability), so the explanation correctly describes what the model sees. Second, for some of the "losing interest" students the decline starts after week 8 and is not yet visible in the data. After refinement (count loss of interest only if it has already manifested): the share of the "activity" group is 0.63 against 0.21 for low-ability students (difference +0.42, CI +0.35 to +0.49), and the share of the "checkpoints" group is 0.16 against 0.57 (−0.41). An explanation cannot find a cause that has not manifested yet, which is a limitation of any explanation.
4. Sanity check: with shuffled labels the contributions almost vanish (mean absolute value 0.004 against 0.052), and the correlation with the real model's contributions is −0.10. Group importance is stable when models are rebuilt on bootstrap samples of students (Spearman rank correlation 0.87, minimum 0.60); the main groups are checkpoints (AUC drop 0.167) and activity (0.086).

## Stage 7 result: fairness

`scripts/stage7_experiment.py` ([docs/stage7_results.txt](docs/stage7_results.txt)); threshold 0.25 (k = 3), week 8, 5 worlds of 2000 students.

- **S0 (no measurement bias):** differences by origin and sex are not significant (false alarms +0.8 pp [−0.6; +2.1] by origin).
- **S3 (students from other regions log in to the LMS less at the same engagement; performance is unaffected):** the failure share is the same across groups (17.7% and 17.8%), but the model flags the other_region group more often: false alarms +2.7 pp [+1.6; +3.9], flag rate +3.2 pp [+0.8; +5.5], failures found +5.0 pp. There is no difference by sex. The model turns measurement bias into decision bias.
- **Mitigation:** per-origin thresholds that equalise the false-alarm rate on validation (0.213 and 0.255) remove the difference (false alarms +0.3 pp [−0.9; +1.4]) with almost no loss of overall accuracy (TPR 0.775 → 0.774, FPR 0.103 → 0.101). But using a group attribute in decisions needs a separate legal and ethical justification, so the mitigation is off by default. Removing the source of the bias (accounting for differences in how groups use the LMS) is preferable.

## First experiment result

`python scripts/stage1_check.py` (output saved in [docs/stage1_results.txt](docs/stage1_results.txt)). Boosting trained on one cohort and tested on another. An excerpt (ROC-AUC):

| week | raw weekly | derived | together |
|---|---|---|---|
| 4 | 0.661 | 0.666 | 0.666 |
| 6 | 0.705 | 0.706 | 0.707 |
| 8 | 0.811 | 0.807 | 0.811 |
| 12 | 0.946 | 0.943 | 0.944 |

**Conclusion: on this simulator, derived features do not improve accuracy over raw weekly values** (a difference of about 0.01, from one pair of cohorts and without confidence intervals, so not significant). The explanation is clear: the outcome in the simulator depends smoothly on engagement, and boosting reconstructs sums and trends from weekly values itself. This neither refutes the hypothesis for real data nor confirms it. For now one can only claim that derived features are **clearer to the instructor** ("3 failed checkpoints in a row" versus "logins in week 7 = 2"); any accuracy gain has to be tested separately.

## Limitations

- Everything measured here comes from **synthetic data**. It shows that the pipeline works, not quality on real students.
- Late weeks (14–16) are almost trivial: by then students who left are already distinguishable by zero activity. Weeks 4–8 are the ones with practical value.
- The multi-course simulator's parameters (about 4% dropout per semester, about 87% admission, grades among admitted students: 2 — 2%, 3 — 23%, 4 — 46%, 5 — 29%) were chosen for plausibility, not from a university's statistics; they should be replaced with department data.
- The stage-1 simulator's parameters were chosen so that the shares of failures (about 26%) and leavers (about 17%) look plausible; they are not estimated from real statistics.
- **Not installed and not used:** FastAPI, SHAP, LightGBM, SQLAlchemy, PyTorch (the development environment had no network): the interface is built on WSGI, explanations are written by hand, and boosting comes from scikit-learn. Neural models (GRU/TCN) were not tried.
- **The interface is a prototype:** sign-in uses signed tokens instead of OAuth2, the secret in demo mode is set in the code, and there is no password or registration; the browser view was checked only through the HTML and SVG, not by eye.
- **PostgreSQL and Docker were not tested** (not in the environment): the schema and `docker-compose.yml` are written but not run; SQLAlchemy is not used because it would not install. The OULAD adapter was not tested on real files: OULAD has no grades 2–5 and no national exam scores, its "courses" are module × semester with a Pass/Fail/Withdrawn result, and "logins" and "minutes" are active days and clicks.
- Because the development environment had no network, LightGBM, SHAP and FastAPI were neither used nor tested; boosting uses scikit-learn's `HistGradientBoostingClassifier`.
